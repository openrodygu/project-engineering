# One-on-ones

A one-on-one is the other person's meeting. It is where you hear what is not in the project tracker.

## Keep it regular

Thirty minutes every week or two is enough for most people. Keep the slot even when there is no crisis. Canceling when busy teaches people that the meeting is optional until something breaks.

## An agenda that works

```markdown
## Their items
- ...

## Status and blockers
- What is moving?
- What is stuck, and what do you need?

## Feedback
- One thing going well.
- One thing to adjust.

## Growth
- What are you learning?
- What do you want next?

## Actions
- ...
- ...
```

Send the agenda before the meeting, but let them add to it first. If they have nothing, ask open questions rather than filling the silence with status updates.

## Questions that open things up

- What is taking more energy than it should?
- What is one thing I could do differently as a lead?
- What are you working on that you are not sure about?
- What would make next week easier?
- Where do you want to be in a year?

Avoid turning the meeting into a task review. The team vault and the issue tracker already hold status.

## Listen more than you talk

A useful rule: the other person should talk for most of the meeting. Your job is to ask, clarify, and remove blockers.

Take notes. Follow up on what you promised. A one-on-one where nothing changes becomes a status meeting people skip.

## Handle the hard topics

If performance, workload, or conflict comes up, do not deflect. Use the feedback structure from [03 Feedback](03-feedback.md). If the issue is bigger than the meeting, name the next step and a date.

## Try it

Write an agenda for your next one-on-one. Leave the first section empty for their items. Add two open questions. After the meeting, write down the actions and check them at the start of the next one.
