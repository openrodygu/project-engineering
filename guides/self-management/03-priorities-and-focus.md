# Priorities and focus

Priority is a choice about what not to do. If everything is high priority, you have no priorities.

## Pick three

A practical daily plan has three outcomes, not twenty. Choose:

1. **One must** — the task that would make the day a failure if it did not happen.
2. **One should** — important, but movable if the day goes sideways.
3. **One could** — useful progress if there is room.

Everything else waits. This is not a rule for every day, but it is a good default.

## Must, should, could

| Level | Test |
| --- | --- |
| **Must** | Someone is waiting, or a commitment breaks without it |
| **Should** | Moves a real outcome this week |
| **Could** | Nice to have, or safe to slip |

Use priority in TaskNotes for the same idea. High priority means "this cycle", not "I feel busy".

## Focus on one thing

Multitasking feels productive because it is busy. It usually costs more than it saves, especially for work that needs thought.

- Set one task to `In progress`.
- Work from the task body, not the whole list.
- Close chat and notifications for the block.
- Finish or reach a clear stopping point before switching.

If five tasks are in progress, nothing is.

## Say no, or say later

You cannot protect focus without declining something. Useful phrases:

- "I can do that Thursday. Does that work?"
- "I can do a short version now, or the full version next week. Which helps?"
- "I am not the right owner for this. Try ____."
- "That is not on the plan this week. Should we trade it for something?"

A clear no is kinder than a vague yes that slips.

## Protect the plan

Plans fail when they meet the first interruption. Leave room for the unexpected:

- Keep one buffer block.
- Put shallow work in batches, not across the whole day.
- Decide what gets dropped when the day is overloaded. Decide before it happens.

## Try it

Look at tomorrow and pick three tasks: one must, one should, one could. Then pick one task on your list to cancel or move to next week. Write one sentence explaining why it can wait.
