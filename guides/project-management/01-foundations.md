# Foundations

Understand what makes work a project, and why a clear outcome beats a busy task list.

## What a project is

A project is temporary work that produces a specific result. It has a start, a finish, and a definition of done. Operations are the ongoing work that keeps things running. Both matter, but they are managed differently.

A project has three constraints that trade against each other:

- **Scope** — what is included.
- **Time** — when it must be finished.
- **Quality** — how good it must be.

Change one and the others move. A shorter deadline usually means less scope or lower quality. Pretending otherwise is how plans fail quietly.

## Start with the outcome

Write the outcome as a change in the world, not as an activity.

- Weak: "Work on the vendor process."
- Strong: "Select a vendor and sign a contract by March 15."

The outcome gives you a finish line. Without one, the work expands until the deadline or the budget stops it.

## Define done

A definition of done is a short checklist that tells everyone when to stop. It is not a feeling. It is observable.

```markdown
**Definition of done:**
- [ ] Vendor comparison covers at least three options
- [ ] Legal has reviewed the contract
- [ ] Purchase order is approved
- [ ] Handover note is written
```

If two people disagree about whether the project is done, the definition is not specific enough.

## Know the constraints

Name the real constraint before planning:

- A fixed deadline with flexible scope.
- A fixed scope with flexible time.
- A fixed budget with flexible scope and time.

Most projects have one constraint that does not move. Say which one it is. Then negotiate the rest in the open.

## Try it

Take three pieces of work from your week.

1. Mark each as **project** or **operations**.
2. For one project, write its outcome in one sentence.
3. Write a definition of done with three to five observable checks.
4. Name the constraint that cannot move.

Put the result in your project brief in `local-project/pm-vault/`.
